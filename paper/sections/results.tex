\section{Results}

\subsection{Networks of fitted cells, three working-memory tasks, a locked protocol}
Every network contains 100 GFR cells drawn from the confirmation cohort (80 Pyr, 10 PV, 5 SST,
5 VIP; Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks}). Each cell keeps its fitted input filters, spike-history filters and
nonlinearity, with time constants from $\sim$0 to 2\,s. Nearly every cell's history filter is
net negative, i.e.\ adapting. Adaptation is strongest in pyramidal cells and weakest in PV cells
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks}d,e). Networks learned three tasks. In the context-dependent decision
(T1-early) the cue arrives with the stimulus. In T1-late it arrives only after the delay. In
delayed match-to-sample (T2-dms) no input before the post-delay probe carries information about
the choice. After 3000 updates every learner was near ceiling on in-distribution trials
(Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:s2}), so all comparisons below use the preregistered primary endpoint:
accuracy on conflict trials at unseen, longer delays (400--600\,ms). All results come from 1,680
runs on the held-out donor cohort (16 paired networks per condition), analysed exactly as locked
before those runs (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{The local rule needs the cells' own spike-history term}
Removing only the spike-history term $\beta$ from the eligibility trace (Eq.~\ref{eq:elig}) lowered
unseen-delay conflict accuracy in all six task-by-optimizer comparisons, by 7--17\pp
(Table~\ref{tab:familyA}, Fig.~\ref{fig:rules}). The difference was significant after Holm
correction in five of them. With Adam, accuracy fell from 81.6\% to 70.6\% (T1-early), from 75.7\%
to 66.5\% (T1-late) and from 80.5\% to 68.1\% (T2-dms). Without $\beta$, the rule's accuracy was close to
or below that of a frozen reservoir that learns only its readout (descriptive). The one comparison that did not survive
correction was T2-dms with RMS normalization ($+7.1$\pp, 95\% CI [1.9, 11.9], Holm $p=0.10$).

The local rule with $\beta$ beat the frozen reservoir in all six comparisons. The gain was
significant in three: T1-late with RMS and T2-dms with either optimizer (+10.6 to +11.9\pp). It
was not significant on T1-early, where the reservoir already reached 76--77\%, nor on T1-late with
Adam (+6.1\pp).
BPTT stayed clearly better than the local rule on the two tasks that require combining stored
information with a later cue: +16.2 and +11.5\pp on T1-late, +14.9 and +22.3\pp on T2-dms (Adam,
RMS). On T1-early the two did not differ.

\subsection{Why: \texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{beta} aligns the local estimate with the true gradient}
The local estimate differs from the exact gradient in two ways. It ignores paths through other
cells, and it uses a random rather than the true feedback vector. Running the same rule with
symmetric feedback isolates the first error. In networks trained with the local rule, the
symmetric-feedback estimate with $\beta$ kept a cosine of 0.76--0.94 with the exact recurrent
gradient at the end of training. Without $\beta$ the cosine was 0.54--0.66 (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}a--c).
Random feedback lowered both curves, and $\beta$ still helped (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}e). Per
postsynaptic pyramidal cell, the cosine with symmetric feedback was 0.74 with $\beta$ and 0.54
without. The improvement from $\beta$ grew with the cell's adaptation loop gain
$|\langle g'\rangle\bar\beta|$. The per-network correlation was $r=0.16$ [0.09, 0.23] (T1-early),
0.23 [0.16, 0.30] (T1-late) and 0.18 [0.11, 0.24] (T2-dms), across 16 networks each
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}d).

Behaviour followed the same split. With symmetric feedback, removing $\beta$ still cost 8.4--12.7\pp
(Family C). Replacing random by symmetric feedback gained only 3.8--4.4\pp (not significant). With
symmetric feedback and $\beta$, the local rule matched BPTT on T1-early ($-0.4$\pp). On T1-late and
T2-dms it still trailed by 12.4 and 11.0\pp. In this setting, therefore, most of the cost of
ignoring a cell's adaptation lies in the eligibility trace itself, while the residual gap to BPTT on
the harder tasks comes from cutting paths through other cells, not from random feedback.

\subsection{Memory sits in the cells; learning shapes the response}
Held-out decoders read the sign of \emph{both} input streams from the delay-period population at
$\ge$98\% accuracy, for every learner including the frozen reservoir (Fig.~\ref{fig:repr}). This held
in T2-dms, where the second stream is a distractor. As designed, the choice could not be decoded in
the T2-dms delay (49--50\%). Learners differed in the response phase, where stored information must be
combined with the cue. In T2-dms, response-phase choice decoding was 99.8\% (BPTT), 98.9\% (local),
92.5\% (no $\beta$) and 83.3\% (reservoir). The slow fitted filters are necessary for the memory:
setting all time constants to $\le$20\,ms (with the static response preserved) dropped every task to
chance (49--50\%; $-26$ to $-32$\pp; Family C).

\subsection{Cell identity and wiring do not matter once the operating point is matched}
In the preregistered factorial, keeping each cell's own parameter combination rather than shuffling
it within type changed the primary endpoint by +1.1 to +4.5\pp (Table~\ref{tab:familyB},
Fig.~\ref{fig:bio}). No effect was significant. Four of six effects were practically negligible (90\% CI
within $\pm5$\pp), and two were inconclusive (T1-late and T2-dms with RMS). The type-prior wiring
versus degree-preserving rewiring was practically negligible in all six comparisons. No identity
$\times$ learner or wiring $\times$ learner interaction was significant.

The operating point is what made earlier versions of this comparison misleading. With the intact
cells' calibration kept (``reference''), shuffled networks fired at 24\,Hz instead of 11--12\,Hz, and
7\% instead of 0.3--0.5\% of their samples were near a cell's ceiling (Fig.~\ref{fig:bio}d--f). At the
tuned budget this did not change accuracy significantly ($+1.8$ to $+3.3$\pp versus matched shuffles).
It does, however, change the regime being compared. Permuting whole parameter sets across types,
with recalibration, cost 1.6--8.1\pp; only T2-dms reached nominal significance ($p=0.003$,
descriptive).

\paragraph{Heterogeneity helps the exact gradient but not the local rule.}
Replacing every cell by its type mean (recalibrated) lowered BPTT accuracy on T1-late by 12.6\pp with
Adam (Holm $p\le0.001$) and on T2-dms by 20.1 and 24.9\pp. Under the local rule, the same manipulation
had no significant effect in any comparison ($-6.0$ to $+1.7$\pp). On T1-early neither learner gained
from heterogeneity. Type-mean networks also fired more slowly (7--8\,Hz).

\subsection{The operating point shapes local learning, task by task}
Moving every cell's calibrated baseline from 2\% to 20\% of its ceiling raised mean rates from about
7\,Hz to 31--35\,Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:op}). BPTT was insensitive: no slope differed from zero. The local
rule was not. Its accuracy fell with higher baselines on T1-early ($-4.6$\pp per doubling, 95\% CI
[$-5.9$, $-3.3$], $p<0.001$), rose on T1-late ($+3.5$\pp, [1.6, 5.6], $p=0.004$) and tended to rise on
T2-dms ($+1.8$\pp, [0.0, 3.5], $p=0.08$). No single operating point was best for local learning
across tasks.
